Evaluation Methodology (SCALE-Sim)

SCALE-Sim configuration used throughout this evaluation
(configs/mx_fused_pe_32x32_os.cfg, restated here for quick reference before the
methodology prose below):
    Array size:        32 x 32 (1024 PEs)
    Dataflow:           output-stationary (os) -- see rationale below
    SRAM:               64 KB each for IFMAP / Filter / OFMAP
    Bandwidth:          nominal 10 (words/cycle); InterfaceBandwidth = CALC, i.e.
                        SCALE-Sim estimates whatever bandwidth avoids stalling
                        compute rather than enforcing a fixed cap -- this is why
                        every workload in Table 1 reports zero stall cycles
    Sparsity support:   disabled
    Per-PE MAC model:   1 op/PE/cycle at N=1 baseline (SCALE-Sim's native

                        per Eq. (1) below, not simulated natively by the tool

To assess the system-level impact of the proposed N-way fused dot-product PE beyond
isolated-cell synthesis results, we use SCALE-Sim, a cycle-level simulator for
systolic-array-based DNN accelerators, to model a 32x32 array (1024 PEs) built from the
proposed compute unit. Each SCALE-Sim PE is mapped to one instance of the fused
dot-product unit, and the array is configured with an output-stationary (OS) dataflow,
consistent with the unit's internally accumulating output register: partial sums are
held and updated locally within each PE across the reduction dimension, matching the
behavior of the double-pumped accumulator that sustains a new fused dot product every
cycle. Because the double-pumped accumulator achieves an initiation interval of one
cycle despite the underlying 4-stage pipeline, the array satisfies SCALE-Sim's default
assumption of one operation issued per PE per cycle at steady state, with the pipeline
depth contributing only a fixed fill/drain overhead per fold, analogous to the systolic
skew already modeled by the tool.

SCALE-Sim's compute engine has no native notion of operand precision or per-PE
reduction width, so each workload is first simulated unmodified on the array to obtain
a baseline, single-element-per-cycle per-layer cycle count together with its stall-cycle
component from the tool's compute report. The compute-bound portion of each layer's
cycle count is then analytically divided by the fusion width N (4, 8, or 16) to project the
cycle count of the fused design, while stall cycles -- which reflect memory/bandwidth-
bound behavior independent of the PE's internal datapath -- are left unchanged. This
preserves SCALE-Sim's memory-traffic accounting, which would otherwise be distorted if
the reduction dimension were instead pre-folded directly in the workload description.
The projected cycle counts are converted to wall-clock latency using the post-synthesis
clock period obtained for each design point (N=4: 0.56 ns; N=8: 0.64 ns; N=16: 0.62 ns), from which
achieved throughput (TOPS) is derived using each workload's total MAC count. Compute-
array power and area are obtained by scaling the post-synthesis per-PE power and area
figures by the number of PEs in the array (1024), and combined with the achieved
throughput to report energy efficiency (TOPS/W) and area efficiency (TOPS/mm^2) under
real workload utilization rather than theoretical peak alone.

The evaluation spans a representative mix of convolutional and transformer-style
workloads -- ResNet-18 and AlexNet for classical CNN inference, ViT-S/B/L/H/bigG,
encoder Transformer forward and input-gradient GEMM kernels, and an MLPerf Transformer
benchmark -- reflecting the primary target domain of MX-format mixed-precision inference
and training. Two workloads (a LLaMA-3B-scale topology and the corresponding
Transformer weight-gradient kernel) were excluded: their largest individual layers
(channel/filter counts in the thousands at 512x512 spatial resolution, and a single
65536x8192x1024 GEMM, respectively) require SCALE-Sim to materialize per-cycle demand
matrices that exceed available host memory, independent of concurrent load. This is a
scaling limitation of trace-based cycle simulation at LLM-scale layer dimensions rather
than a property of the proposed PE, and is noted here as a stated limitation of the
simulation methodology. Separately, the MLPerf Transformer benchmark decomposes
multi-head attention into several hundred narrow per-head GEMMs, most of which occupy a
single row/column of the 32x32 array; its low reported utilization therefore reflects
that decomposition rather than the fused PE's efficiency, and it is retained in Table 1
for completeness with this caveat noted.

Post-Processing Methodology: Mapping SCALE-Sim Cycles to the N-way Fused Design

SCALE-Sim itself simulates only a baseline PE that issues one scalar operation per
cycle; it has no notion of a PE that fuses N reduction elements into a single
pipelined dot product. The mapping from this baseline simulation to the proposed
design is therefore performed as a deterministic post-processing step, kept separate
from the simulation itself so it can be re-evaluated against revised synthesis results
without re-running SCALE-Sim.

For each workload, SCALE-Sim's compute report gives, per layer l, a total cycle count
C_l and a stall cycle count S_l (C_l >= S_l), where C_l - S_l is the compute-bound
portion of the layer's runtime and S_l is the portion attributable to memory/bandwidth
limits. Because stall cycles are independent of the PE's internal reduction width,
only the compute-bound portion is rescaled by the fusion width N:

    C_l(N) = S_l + ceil( (C_l - S_l) / N )                                 (1)

The workload-level cycle count is C(N) = sum over l of C_l(N), computed per layer
before summing -- using each layer's own stall/compute split -- rather than aggregating
cycles first and rescaling once, so the ceiling rounding is not amortized across layers
of very different sizes.

Given the post-synthesis clock period T(N) for a design point (T(4) = 0.56 ns,
T(8) = 0.64 ns, T(16) = 0.62 ns) and P = 1024 PEs in the array:

    Latency(N)       = C(N) x T(N)                                        (2)
    Achieved TOPS(N) = 2 x MACs_workload / Latency(N)                      (3)
    Peak TOPS(N)     = 2 x N x P x (1 / T(N))                             (4)
    Utilization(N)   = Achieved TOPS(N) / Peak TOPS(N)                    (5)

MACs_workload is the workload's total multiply-accumulate count, obtained directly
from SCALE-Sim's own topology hyper-parameter calculation
(topologies.get_all_mac_ops()) rather than recomputed independently, so it is
guaranteed consistent with the exact layer dimensions SCALE-Sim simulated.

Power and area are not modeled by SCALE-Sim and are introduced entirely in this
post-processing step from the post-synthesis per-PE figures Area_PE(N) and
Power_PE(N):

    Power_array(N) = Power_PE(N) x P                                      (6)
    Area_array(N)  = Area_PE(N) x P / 1e6            [um^2 -> mm^2]        (7)
    TOPS/W(N)      = Achieved TOPS(N) / Power_array(N)                    (8)
    TOPS/mm^2(N)   = Achieved TOPS(N) / Area_array(N)                     (9)

This is implemented as two decoupled stages: (1) a one-time extraction of per-workload
{MACs_workload, C_l, S_l} from SCALE-Sim's output into a synthesis-independent
raw-metrics file (mx_pe_eval_raw_metrics.csv), and (2) a lightweight recomputation step
(recompute_ppa.py) that applies equations (1)-(9) using only that file and the current
per-PE synthesis figures. Stage 2 can be rerun with revised frequency, power, or area
numbers -- or additional fusion widths N -- without repeating stage 1.

Why the Fusion Helps: Realized Speedup and the Cost of Widening N

Two distinct effects contribute to the results in Table 1, and are separated here so
each can be checked independently: (i) whether the N-way fusion delivers its full
theoretical cycle-count reduction on real workloads once scheduled by SCALE-Sim, and
(ii) whether widening the fusion -- N=4 to N=8, and N=8 to N=16 -- is a net win once
the accompanying change in clock period, power, and area is accounted for.

(i) Realized speedup over an unfused, same-clock reference. SCALE-Sim's own baseline
run -- one scalar operation issued per PE per cycle -- is exactly the N=1 case of
equation (1). Re-clocking that same baseline cycle count at each fused design's own
clock period (0.56 ns, 0.64 ns, or 0.62 ns) gives a same-clock, unfused reference point
that isolates the effect of fusion alone, with the array size, dataflow, and workload
held fixed. This reference is analytical (it re-uses the already-simulated N=1 cycle
count at a hypothetical clock; it is not an independently synthesized N=1 PE), and is
reported as such -- not as a competing design. Concretely for ResNet-18: the same-clock
N=1 reference achieves 3.06 TOPS at 0.56 ns, 2.68 TOPS at 0.64 ns, and 2.76 TOPS at
0.62 ns, against 12.23, 21.40, and 44.19 TOPS for the fused design at N=4, N=8, and
N=16 respectively -- exactly 4.00x, 8.00x, and 16.00x. This exact ratio holds for every
evaluated workload at every N:

    Workload              N=4 ratio   N=8 ratio   N=16 ratio
    ResNet-18               4.00x       8.00x       16.00x
    AlexNet                 4.00x       8.00x       16.00x
    Transformer-Fwd         4.00x       8.00x       16.00x
    Transformer-InpGrad     4.00x       8.00x       16.00x
    MLPerf-Transformer*     4.00x       8.00x       16.00x
    ViT-S                   4.00x       8.00x       16.00x
    ViT-B                   4.00x       8.00x       16.00x
    ViT-L                   4.00x       8.00x       16.00x
    ViT-H                   4.00x       8.00x       16.00x
    ViT-bigG                4.00x       8.00x       16.00x

The exact ratio (rather than something below N, as would appear if fusion introduced
scheduling overhead or if memory bandwidth capped throughput before compute did)
follows directly from two properties already established above: the fused design
retains SCALE-Sim's one-issue-per-PE-per-cycle assumption at steady state (so the
projection in equation (1) is exact whenever stall cycles are zero), and every
evaluated workload is compute-bound end-to-end (zero stall cycles, Table 1). The
practical claim this supports is that the fusion benefit is not merely a peak-throughput
figure -- it is fully realized on real, scheduled DNN and transformer workloads, and
remains exact as the fusion width grows from 4 to 16.

(ii) Is widening the fusion worth its cost? Unlike (i), this comparison uses
independently synthesized design points, so the outcome is not fixed by construction,
and it must be checked separately at each step.

N=4 to N=8: clock period grows from 0.56 ns to 0.64 ns (14.3% lower frequency, the
expected cost of a deeper fused reduction tree), per-PE power grows 1.41x, and per-PE
area grows 1.34x. Doubling the fusion width nonetheless grows peak array throughput by
1.75x (2x width against the 1.143x frequency penalty), enough to outrun both cost
increases: TOPS/W improves by 1.75/1.41 = 1.24x and TOPS/mm^2 improves by
1.75/1.34 = 1.31x, matching the per-workload figures in Table 1 (e.g. ResNet-18:
2.11/1.70 = 1.24x on TOPS/W, 1.38/1.06 = 1.30x on TOPS/mm^2).

N=8 to N=16: notably, the clock period here improves slightly rather than worsening --
0.64 ns to 0.62 ns, a 3.2% higher frequency -- while per-PE power grows 1.52x and
per-PE area grows 1.32x. This is the opposite clock trend from the N=4-to-N=8 step, and
is reported here purely as an observation from the supplied synthesis figures: nothing
in the SCALE-Sim-side methodology explains or assumes it, and it would need the
underlying RTL/timing analysis to attribute (e.g. a re-pipelined or better-balanced
N=16 reduction tree) if that explanation is wanted in the paper. Regardless of cause,
its effect on the comparison is favorable: doubling the width again now grows peak
throughput by 2.06x (a 2x width bonus plus the small frequency gain), which outruns
both cost increases even more comfortably than the previous step -- TOPS/W improves by
2.06/1.52 = 1.36x and TOPS/mm^2 improves by 2.06/1.32 = 1.57x.

Over the full range evaluated (N=4 to N=16), per-PE power grows 2.15x and area grows
1.76x, while peak throughput grows 3.61x (slightly below the ideal 4x, since N=16's
clock is marginally slower than N=4's despite being faster than N=8's) -- still enough
for a net 1.68x improvement in TOPS/W and 2.05x in TOPS/mm^2. At all three synthesized
points evaluated, wider fusion is a net win on every efficiency metric, not only on raw
throughput, and the win grows larger at each step rather than tapering off.
